# 0044. Circle calls: live audio and video between members, kept nowhere

- Date: 2026-11-02
- Status: Accepted (2026-11-11), amended 2026-11-11 (§9: addresses from a STUN server, with the Circles connection as the fallback path, from the transport check), amended 2026-11-13 (§4: private room, Call in the direct conversation's header, ring details; §4 and §5: ring alerts outside the window, the call view opening on join)
- Parent: [0042](0042-circle-chats.md)
- Amends: [0042](0042-circle-chats.md) §3 (a `call` field on `chat-post` and `chat-edit` at format 2)
- Related: [0032](0032-circles.md), [0043](0043-circles-live-in-the-chat.md), [circles plan](../circles-plan.md) (peer signals, camera and microphone), [Circle calls spec](../circle-calls-ux-study/spec.md)

## Context

Two pieces calls need already exist. [Peer signals](../circles-plan.md#peer-signals) carry short messages between members, to everyone in a Circle or to one identity. The receiver takes the sender's identity from the connection, so a signal can't be forged by another device. Payloads are at most 4 KB of JSON, at 30 a second per peer with bursts of 60, and only main can send or listen today. [Camera and microphone](../circles-plan.md#camera-and-microphone) access is granted to the app's own page and nothing else, and macOS asks once per device. Nothing in the app uses either for calls yet.

Since ADR 0042 §10 and 0043, a Circle is a place in the chat with channels and people, and a person's panel there is about membership. Direct messages inside a Circle are deferred (0042 §14) because anything in the Circle's log can be read by every member. A call stores nothing, so it doesn't have that problem.

There is no server. Media has to go between members' Macs. WebRTC does that with echo cancellation, jitter buffers and codecs built in, and needs only an exchange of session descriptions and network candidates, which peer signals can carry. Without a relay (TURN), two Macs behind strict NATs can't connect; the circles plan already lists TURN as an open question. A session description with audio and video is often larger than 4 KB.

## Decision

1. **Media is WebRTC between members' Macs, set up over peer signals.** Offers, answers and candidates go on `call.*` topics, addressed to one identity. A message larger than one signal is compressed and, if still too large, split into numbered parts the receiver reassembles. The renderer runs the peer connections; main relays its call signals over new IPC, and only `call.*` topics cross. Media is encrypted by WebRTC's DTLS-SRTP, and because the fingerprints travel in signals the receiver attributes to a member's device, a third party can't sit in the middle.
2. **A call is a room with a mesh.** Each person in a call connects to every other. A room has a name chosen by whoever starts it: a channel's call is `channel/<id>`, a call with one person is a random id, and other screens can name their own rooms. While in a call, each device sends a `call.state` signal every few seconds with the room, whether it's muted and whether its camera is on; a device that stops sending for 15 seconds has left. Video is on for up to 6 people. A seventh person joins with audio only, and the call view says why.
3. **Calls in channels.** A Circle channel's header has **Call**. Anyone in the Circle can start or join a channel's call. Starting one posts a message to the channel with a `call` field (decision 7), which shows as "Maya started a call" with **Join** while it's live. While a channel has a live call, its row in the rail shows a live mark with the faces of who's in it, and the switcher's menu shows the mark on the Circle. Nobody is rung.
4. **Calls with one person.** A person's panel in a Circle has **Call**, as does their row in the rail's People section on hover and the header of the direct conversation with them, while they are online. The call's room is `person/<16 hex>`, and its `call.state` goes to the other person only; a session in that room ignores anyone else, so the rest of the Circle can't tell who is calling whom. The caller sends `call.ring` to the person's identity every 5 seconds for up to 30 seconds, and every device of the person rings with an in-app card on any screen, with **Join** and **Decline**. While Boundary's window isn't in front, the ring also shows a system notification with **Answer** and **Decline**, bounces the Dock and plays a repeating ring drawn in the app; all three stop when the ring ends or the window comes to the front, and each ring gets one notification. The page owns the rings and sends main the list; main decides from the window's state whether to alert. A Mac with Boundary's window closed doesn't ring. **Join** sends `answering`, which extends the caller's wait to 60 seconds so the person can choose devices; joining on one device stops the person's other devices, and **Decline** makes the caller cancel. A person already in a call answers `busy`, and the caller sees "Sam is in another call". The caller sees "No answer" after the wait, or "Sam declined", each with **Try again** and **Close**. A call joined from a ring ends if the caller hasn't shown within 20 seconds, and a call with one person ends when the other person leaves once they have been in the room, connected or not. **Call** on someone who is already ringing this user joins their call instead of ringing back. If both press **Call** before either ring arrives, each side sees the other's ring while still calling, and the side whose identity sorts higher joins the other's room instead of answering `busy`. During a call, the person it's with has no **Call**, and **Call** for anyone else or **Join** on another ring asks "Leave the call with Sam?" first. Main relays a private room's `call.state` only to the person named. Main passes the sending device with each call event, and the call tunnel opens to that device. Nothing about the call enters the Circle's log, so it's as private as the two Macs. A Mac on an older build drops `call.ring`, and the caller sees "No answer".
5. **The call stays with you.** While in a call, a call bar sits at the foot of the chat rail in every place, with the call's name, mute, camera and **Leave**, and the Chat icon in the icon rail shows a live dot. On other screens the same bar floats at the bottom left. Clicking the bar opens the call view, as do placing a call and answering a ring: tiles in the main area and, for a channel's call, the channel's chat beside them in the compact thread (Circle chats spec phase 7).
6. **Joining asks first.** **Join** and **Call** open a join sheet: a camera preview, microphone and camera pickers, the camera off by default, and **Join**. The sheet asks macOS for a device only when it's about to be used. A denied device shows a line that links to System Settings, and the user can join without it. Default devices and whether the camera starts on are in Settings, under **Calls**.
7. **Format 2 reserves a call marker.** `chat-post` and `chat-edit` take an optional `call` of `{ id, ended? }`, where `id` is the room's 16 to 32 hex characters and `ended` is the author's clock in milliseconds. A post with `call` may have empty text. Only the author edits, as for any message, so the starter's device adds `ended` when it sees the room empty; if it can't, readers show the call as over once no `call.state` for it arrives, without a length. This rides ADR 0042's format 2, so it costs no format of its own.
8. **What a call never does.** No recording, no transcript, no agents in calls, and no screen sharing in this ADR. The page may only use `media` (camera and microphone); screen capture would need its own permission decision.

9. **Addresses come from a STUN server, and the Circles connection carries what can't go direct.** Each peer connection offers its host candidates, server-reflexive candidates from `stun.cloudflare.com`, and one more candidate at the lowest priority: a UDP port on `127.0.0.1` that the Circles network process opens for that call and carries over the existing Circles connection to that member (the call tunnel). ICE prefers a direct pair and uses the tunnel only when none works, so a call connects wherever the two members' Circles connection does. Hyperswarm's view of a Mac's address isn't used: it never connected across networks. The tunnel only accepts packets from the first local sender, only for a current member, and at most 8 tunnels per peer; media stays encrypted end to end by DTLS-SRTP, so the network process carries packets it can't read. Settings can turn the STUN server off (spec phase 5), which leaves calls on local addresses and the tunnel.

## Consequences

- Two members who have a Circles connection can always call, without a relay of their own. Members with no Circles connection between them (both behind randomizing NATs) can't, and the call view names who can't be reached instead of failing quietly. A relay for the Circles connection would help both calls and sync, and needs its own decision.
- The STUN server learns each member's public address and when they call. Settings says it's used and can turn it off, at the cost of every call across networks going through the tunnel.
- A call through the tunnel crosses the Circles network process twice per packet. In the check that cost about 5% CPU and at most 3 ms of timer lag with five video streams. libudx sends these packets outside the Circles stream's congestion window, so only WebRTC's own congestion control slows a call down. Whether a busy call slows Circle sync on a thin link is not yet measured.
- On a congested or throttled link, WebRTC lowers video only down to its own minimum; below that, packets are lost. On a hotspot throttled to roughly 128 kbps, video through the tunnel lost more than half its packets. Quality steps below WebRTC's minimum would be a separate change.
- A mesh sends each person's video to every other person, so upload grows with the call. Five video streams each way held 30 frames a second on an M1 over a phone hotspot, so the 6-person video cap stays.
- Peer signals gain a renderer path for `call.*` topics, and their rate limit now covers call setup; a burst of candidates must stay under it.
- The chat's who-lines and live marks depend on `call.state` signals, which a member who is offline never receives, so a call's length in the channel is best-effort.
- Another screen can run calls in rooms it names, with its own rules for who connects, through the same call service, the way it can mount a Circle channel through `CircleThread`.

## Transport check results

The spec's phase 1 check made real connections between two Macs, with generated media, in five ways of finding addresses. Two runs, each with one Mac on home broadband and the other on a phone hotspot:

| | First run (hotspot throttled) | Second run (hotspot at about 2.4 Mbps) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local addresses | None connected | 0 of 3 |
| Hyperswarm's address | None connected | 0 of 3 |
| STUN server | 3 of 3 from broadband, 0 of 3 from the hotspot | 3 of 3 from the hotspot |
| Circles connection (tunnel) | 12 of 12, both ends | 3 of 3 |
| STUN, then tunnel | Connected every time; through the tunnel when STUN failed, after 7 to 11 s | 3 of 3, all direct |

In the second run, video loads of five streams each way, through STUN and through the tunnel, held about 30 frames a second with at most 0.7% loss. A three-minute call through the tunnel carried 36,000 packets each way and lost one, with no path changes, and audio delay stayed between 119 and 132 ms. In two of the six tunnel load measurements one side's audio delay was near 200 ms, above the 150 ms that's comfortable for talking. In the first run the throttled hotspot lost more than half of tunnel video packets, while audio over the same tunnel lost none, and on one Wi-Fi the same five-stream load lost nothing.
